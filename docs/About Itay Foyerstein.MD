THE PUSH METHODOLOGY
The Push Methodology

The Push is a practical operating model for technical leaders who have become the dependency path for their own team.

It is designed for the moment when a leader realizes:

“The team is moving, but too much of that movement still depends on me.”

The problem is not usually lack of commitment.

It is often the opposite.

The leader is highly involved, highly responsible, and highly effective.

But the organization has started to rely on that effectiveness as infrastructure.

The Push helps change that.

The problem: the leader has become the system

A technical leader becomes a bottleneck when too many important activities continue to route through them:

final decisions
approvals
technical judgment
escalation handling
prioritization
stakeholder alignment
quality control
rescue work

At first, this looks like strong leadership.

Over time, it produces three costs:

The team stops building judgment

People learn to escalate instead of deciding.

The leader loses strategic capacity

Time that should go into direction, talent, architecture, organizational design, and long-term priorities is consumed by immediate execution.

The organization becomes fragile

Progress depends on one person’s availability, attention, and intervention.

The Push addresses the operating model behind this pattern.

Stage 1: Diagnose the dependency

The first step is to identify where the leader has become the default route for progress.

We examine:

which decisions keep returning
which approvals are unnecessary
where ownership is unclear
where people wait instead of act
which work only moves when the leader intervenes
which tasks the leader continues to carry because “it is faster”

This is the Player Trap.

The leader is still playing the role that originally made them successful, even though the organization now needs them to build capacity in others.

The objective is not to blame the leader.

It is to make the dependency visible.

Stage 2: Expose the Invisible Executor

Many leaders carry work that does not appear in any role description.

They connect people, resolve ambiguity, review decisions, protect quality, translate strategy, calm stakeholders, and keep delivery moving.

This hidden work makes them the Invisible Executor.

Because the work is invisible, it is difficult to transfer.

The Push makes that load explicit:

what the leader is actually doing
why the work returns to them
what judgment is embedded in it
what can be transferred
what must remain leadership-owned

This creates a real map of the operating system around the team.

Stage 3: Clarify decision rights

Delegation often fails because leaders transfer tasks without transferring decision authority.

The team receives more responsibility, but the leader still remains the final approval point.

The Push separates:

decisions the team can make independently
decisions that require consultation
decisions that require escalation
decisions that should remain with the leader
decisions that need clearer principles rather than more supervision

This reduces ambiguity without removing control.

Stage 4: Transfer judgment, not only work

The purpose is not to push more tasks downward.

It is to build the team’s ability to make better decisions.

That requires:

clear principles
decision boundaries
visible trade-offs
structured review
feedback on judgment
gradual expansion of ownership

The leader moves from being the answer to improving how answers are produced.

Stage 5: Build strategic leadership capacity

As dependency decreases, leadership capacity increases.

The leader can spend more time on:

strategic direction
organization design
management quality
cross-functional alignment
architecture and technical leverage
AI governance
future capability
the decisions only they should make

The outcome is not simply “more delegation.”

It is a stronger operating system.

Why ordinary delegation often fails

Many leaders already know they should delegate.

The issue is not awareness.

The issue is that the work contains context, judgment, relationships, and risk that cannot simply be handed over.

The Push treats delegation as an operating-model transition.

It asks:

What knowledge is currently trapped in the leader?
What decision criteria are implicit?
What risk is the leader protecting against?
What capability must exist before ownership can move?
What feedback loop will allow the team to improve?

This is why the method works inside real work, not outside it.
