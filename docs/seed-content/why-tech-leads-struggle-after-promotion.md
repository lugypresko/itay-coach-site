# Why tech leads struggle after promotion

## Short answer

Tech Leads often struggle after promotion because the job changes faster than the leadership model they are using—and the skills that made them successful as individual contributors don't translate directly to management.

## Key takeaways

- The role shifts from execution to delegation and alignment.
- Execution habits can stay ahead of leadership habits.
- The Push coaching pillar provides the framework for the transition.

## Citation snippet

Tech Leads struggle after promotion when execution habits run ahead of leadership habits, and the operating model isn't yet clear.

## Content

Promoting a great Tech Lead to Engineering Manager is logical—they understand the technical domain, they've earned the team's respect, they ship high-quality work. But the transition is harder than it looks.

**Why the transition is difficult:**
Tech Leads excel at execution: solving hard technical problems, shipping features, removing blockers, making code better. These are exactly the skills that earned them the promotion. But Engineering Management is a different job:
- **Not about personal output:** As a Tech Lead, you could ship high-quality code yourself. As a manager, your impact comes through your team, not through your code.
- **Not about technical decisions alone:** As a Tech Lead, "What's the right architecture?" might be your decision. As a manager, "Who should make this decision?" and "How should we decide?" matter more.
- **Not about staying close to the code:** As a Tech Lead, staying close to the code kept you current and gave you credibility. As a manager, deep code involvement can become a bottleneck.

**The common struggle pattern:**
1. **First days:** Everything feels normal. You're now a manager, but you're still doing tech lead work—reviewing code, helping with technical decisions, staying close to the details.
2. **First weeks:** The job expands. You have 1:1s, planning meetings, roadmap discussions, hiring conversations. But you're still carrying your tech lead responsibilities.
3. **First month:** You're overloaded. You're trying to do both jobs at once—be a technical authority and be a manager. Something has to give.
4. **After three months:** You're either burned out or you've defaulted to tech lead work (abandoning management duties) or management work (losing technical credibility). Either way, the transition is painful.

**Where execution habits become a problem:**
As a Tech Lead, you could solve problems directly—jump into the code, fix the issue, move on. As a manager, your job is to help others solve problems. This requires:
- **Explaining your thinking:** Not just showing the answer, but explaining how you think about problems.
- **Asking good questions:** Rather than jumping to solutions, helping the team think through problems.
- **Creating frameworks:** Building decision processes that the team can apply, not just solving individual problems.
- **Stepping back:** Letting people fail and learn, not preventing failure through your technical involvement.

These are leadership habits. They're different from execution habits.

**The promotion transition framework:**
The Invisible Executor → Trusted Operator → Strategic Leader progression shows the path:
1. **Invisible Executor:** You're still doing both roles—executing and managing. Output is strong, but your leadership logic is hidden.
2. **Trusted Operator:** You've made your management decision frameworks visible. The team understands how you think about delegation, priorities, and leadership. You're reliable and predictable.
3. **Strategic Leader:** With a clear operating model in place, you can shape direction, develop the team, and have strategic conversations about organizational goals.

Most newly promoted managers struggle in the Invisible Executor phase. The transition to Trusted Operator requires making your leadership model explicit.

**What helps the transition:**
1. **Acknowledge the job change:** Accept that management is not a tech lead role with extra duties. It's a different job with different success metrics.
2. **Make your leadership thinking visible:** Document how you think about delegation, decision-making, and prioritization. Share this with the team.
3. **Set boundaries:** Decide what you will and won't do technically. Maybe you stay hands-on with one high-risk area, but step back from most code review.
4. **Focus on team development:** Invest in helping individuals grow, not in personal technical output. That's where your impact happens now.
5. **Get support:** Find a coach or mentor who can help you navigate the transition. This is common enough that help is available.

## FAQ

### Why do tech leads struggle after promotion?

Because the job changes fundamentally. Tech leads succeed by executing well. Managers succeed by multiplying their team's capability through clarity, delegation, and vision. These are different skills, and the transition requires both acknowledging the change and building new habits.

### How long does the transition usually take?

The most difficult phase is the first three to six months, when you're trying to do both jobs. If you make the explicit shift to management (letting go of tech lead responsibilities) and build a clear operating model, the transition stabilizes around six months in.

### Can I stay hands-on with code as an Engineering Manager?

Yes, but carefully. Stay hands-on with something specific and high-value, not everything. Be explicit about what you're doing and why. And ensure that code involvement doesn't become a bottleneck for the team.

### What should I stop doing as a manager that I did as a Tech Lead?

You should probably stop: reviewing every pull request, being the default answer for technical questions, being the person who fixes production issues, being the go-to person for architecture decisions. These become bottlenecks. Instead, build frameworks so the team can decide.

### How do I know if I've made the transition successfully?

Your team makes most decisions without waiting for you. Technical quality is maintained without your personal involvement. People understand your leadership thinking, not just your technical thinking. You're doing strategic work, not just operational work.

### What if I realize I prefer being a Tech Lead?

That's valid. The two roles require different skills and mindsets. Some great Tech Leads prefer staying in that role rather than moving into management. It's a choice, not a failure.

### What's the difference between a Tech Lead and an Engineering Manager with this framework?

Tech Lead focuses on technical excellence and direct execution. Engineering Manager focuses on team capability, clarity, and delegation. The Invisible Executor framework describes the problem: new managers trying to do both. Trusted Operator is the solution: clear delegation and operating rules that free the manager to manage.
